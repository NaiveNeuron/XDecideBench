XDecideBench

Evaluating Typed Decision Models and Confidence Policies Across Languages

Marek Šuppa (Comenius University in Bratislava, Cisco Systems) and Jaroslav Kopčan (Kempelen Institute of Intelligent Technologies)

  • Paper on arXiv
  • PDF
  • GitHub
  • Cite

XDecideBench is a multilingual benchmark for Typed Decision Models: models that return a probability for each answer an application declares, such as one of k intents, yes or no, or a level on an ordinal rubric. It asks whether model rankings hold across tasks and languages, how much open decision models gain over the Qwen backbones they were fine-tuned from, and whether a confidence threshold fitted on English data keeps its error rate in other languages.

  • 9 suites
  • 122 languages
  • 88,425 texts
  • 153,875 typed questions
  • 11 Typed Decision Models

The code, manifests and scorer are not public yet. They will be released in the GitHub repository.

Abstract

Applications use Typed Decision Models to route and moderate text in many languages, and they often act only on decisions above a confidence threshold. We introduce XDecideBench, a benchmark of nine suites in 122 languages with 88,425 texts and 153,875 typed questions. All suites share one scoring contract, and we report aligned, locally labelled and native items separately. Model rankings change from suite to suite. The model that is best in English, chosen in hindsight, loses significantly in about a quarter of the suite-language cells, and on Burmese Belebele it falls far behind the best model. Three open models fine-tuned for typed decisions beat the Qwen models they were built from in all twelve development languages. When the Qwen models are run locally, a pre-registered held-out cohort confirms that the accuracy gain is larger in the four languages with the least web text than in the six with the most. In both sets of languages, however, fine-tuning fixes a similar share of the base model's errors, so the larger gain may reflect weaker base models. A confidence threshold set on English data to allow 5% errors exceeds that error rate on XNLI, even in German and French.

Key findings

Rankings change from suite to suite

Kendall τ between the Core12 ranking and the rankings on the other suites is only .33 (RTP-LX) to .64 (NLI, Injongo). GPT-6 Luna is eighth on Core12 but first on the native INCLUDE exams.

Choosing a model on English is risky

If we pick the best English model on each parallel suite, another model beats it significantly in 56 of 232 suite–language cells. On Belebele, Microsoft's Decision-1 is best in English but loses significantly in 37 of 105 languages. In Burmese it reaches Brier skill .336, against .814 for Jev.

Open decision heads beat their own backbones

Clef, Clef-flash and pplx-decider beat their Qwen backbones in all twelve Core12 languages, by 7.6 to 18.2 accuracy points under the local readout. A pre-registered held-out cohort confirms that the accuracy gain is larger in the four languages with the least web text than in the six with the most. In both groups the heads fix a similar share of the backbone's errors, so the larger gain may reflect weaker backbones.

English-fitted confidence thresholds do not transfer

A threshold set on English data for 5% errors admits 7.8–12.4% errors on German and French XNLI, and more in low-web-share languages. On MASSIVE, error rates stay near target, but the models admit fewer decisions: Jev admits 10.8% of Swahili decisions against 46.8% of English ones.

How models are scored

Each request declares its answers, and the model returns a probability for each. A suite is scored only when a submission covers every case. Refusals, interface limits and invalid outputs count as a uniform distribution, and the share of valid outputs is reported beside each score. The metric is the Brier skill score, BSS = 1 − BS(model) / BS(reference), with uniform chance as the reference for choice decisions and the cell's base rate for skewed yes/no and score decisions. Every mean has a 95% cluster-bootstrap interval. To test confidence policies, a risk threshold for 5% errors and a split conformal set are fitted on English data and applied in other languages.

How XDecideBench differs from other decision-model benchmarks

Earlier studies evaluate one Typed Decision Model across languages, or several models in English. Deußer et al. evaluate Jev alone on 37 datasets in many languages. JevBench, S1MB and Decision Index compare several models in English, and system-one-benchmark compares five models in eight languages on choice decisions only. XDecideBench runs eleven models on the same multilingual items for all three decision types, reports aligned and native evidence separately, pairs open heads with their own backbones, and tests whether English-fitted confidence policies transfer across languages.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a benchmark for Typed Decision Models such as Jev in languages other than English?
Yes. XDecideBench scores Jev and ten other Typed Decision Models on the same items in 122 languages. Jev is second on Core12 and best on Wide-Belebele, but below the base rate on RTP-LX and BRIGHTER.
Which decision model is best for multilingual classification or routing?
No single model is best everywhere. pplx-decider leads Core12, Jev leads Wide-Belebele, Clef-flash leads MASSIVE intent routing and GPT-6 Luna leads the native INCLUDE exams. The paper recommends choosing on suites that match your decision type and setting.
Can I choose a model on English results and deploy it in other languages?
Not safely. The best model in English loses significantly in about a quarter of suite–language cells.
Can I fit a confidence threshold on English data and reuse it in other languages?
Not without checking. A threshold set for 5% errors on English admits 7.8–12.4% errors on German and French XNLI, and more in languages with less web text. On MASSIVE it keeps the error rate but admits fewer decisions.
How does XDecideBench handle low-resource languages?
Wide-Belebele (106 languages), the NLI family (37, including AfriXNLI and AmericasNLI) and InjongoIntent (16 African languages) reach languages with very little web text. The pre-registered head–backbone analysis covers only the twelve Core12 languages, and only Swahili among them lies below tier 3 of the Joshi et al. taxonomy.
Do fine-tuned decision models beat the LLMs they were built from?
In this panel, yes. Clef, Clef-flash and pplx-decider beat their Qwen backbones in all twelve Core12 languages, with larger accuracy gains in low-web-share languages but a similar share of errors fixed.
Can I score my own model?
Not yet. The scorer and manifests will be released in the GitHub repository.
